# Ten drills, for Coach Kalinin to approve or reject

**Who this is for:** Aleksandr, this is for you. Nobody with coaching expertise has
looked at these ten drills yet, and the app will not use any of them until somebody
with your background says yes. Your yes or no on each one is the only thing missing.

**What this document is:** the ten drills written out in plain words, so you never
have to open a file. Everything below was read out of the drill files themselves.
Where a drill says something specific — 20 repetitions, 6 metres, 18 out of 20 — that
number is what is written down, not a summary of it.

**What you are being asked:** for each drill, "yes, that is sound coaching" or
"no, and here is what is wrong with it". Eight yes and two no is a perfectly good
answer. You do not have to fix anything — a no simply sends the drill back.

**One thing this document is not:** a coaching opinion. Everything marked
**Worth a second look** below was spotted by a non-coach reading the files against
each other. Some of it will be nothing. That is expected; the point is only that you
see it before you sign, rather than after.

## Read this first — five things that are true of the whole set

These apply across the drills, so they are said once here instead of ten times.

**1. Four of the ten lean on the same one study.** *Serve plus one*,
*Cross-court battle*, *Short ball: attack and close* and *Forehand depth ladder* all
justify their key number with one 2023 paper analysing **elite men's singles**
matches. Each drill quotes it for something different (rally length, where points
finish, where finishing shots land). Four of these drills are offered to under-14s,
and to girls as well as boys. Whether a number taken from professional men's tennis
should set the threshold for a 14-year-old is a coaching judgement, and it is yours,
not the paper's.

**2. "Reviewed" is a status, not a signature.** All ten are marked `reviewed`, which
in this project means an automated check passed — the shape of the file, the links
working, no copied text, the tags spelled correctly. No person is named anywhere in
any of the ten files as having reviewed them. Every one was written by the same
automated author, and every source was fetched on the same day, 4 September 2026.

**3. The pictures fold onto one side of the net, and can mislead.** Six drills carry
a diagram. Four of them are drawn as a **half court**, and the rules of the drawing
system force everything in a half-court picture onto one side of the net. So in
*Split-step reaction gate* the words say the feeder stands at "the opposite service
line" while the picture puts him on the player's own side; in *Pre-serve routine
anchor* the serve targets are drawn in the server's own service boxes rather than
across the net. Nothing is wrong with the coaching there — it is a drawing
convention. But do not use the pictures to judge which side of the net anything is
on. The words are what count.

**4. One drill is a hard "supervised only".** *Bodyweight court circuit* is flagged
in the file as requiring a qualified strength-and-conditioning professional to be
present. That is not advisory wording — the app will not place that drill in a
session unless the flag is set, and the flag is set. If you approve it, you are
approving a drill that says an S&C professional must be there.

**5. Sources: what the file actually records.** Each drill lists its sources. There
are two kinds:

- **An in-house entry** — "TennisAI coaching library … written from standard
  coaching curricula", with no link. This is the project saying "we wrote this
  ourselves from ordinary coaching practice". All ten have one.
- **An outside source** — a named author or body, a title, and a link that was
  opened. Each of these carries a date the page was read (`fetchedAt`), and all ten
  drills record **4 September 2026**.

The one caveat you should know: the automated check insists a `reviewed` drill has at
least one working outside link, but it does **not** check that anybody recorded a date
for actually reading it. In these ten they all did. It is a courtesy, not a guarantee.

## 1. Split-step reaction gate

- **Category:** footwork. **Level:** all four, beginner to high performance.
- **Ages:** under-12 up to adult.
- **Length:** 8 minutes by default; the app may run it anywhere from 5 to 15.
- **Volume:** 20 repetitions, 3 sets, 40 seconds' rest. Medium intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 6 players, half a court.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones.
- **What the player does:** stands on the centre mark. Two cones sit 3 m either side
  of it, one step inside the baseline. A feeder feeds to one side without saying
  which. As the feeder starts the swing, the player hops into a split step, pushes
  off the outside foot, reaches the cone on the fed side, and recovers to the centre
  mark. Twenty times, then rest.
- **What it develops:** landing the split step *before* the opponent's contact, first
  step off the outside foot, recovery to the middle. Success is 18 of 20 split steps
  landing before the feeder hits, with a controlled arrival at the cone.
- **Progression:** feed later and wider, then swap the feed for a live rally where
  only split steps that land in time are counted. **Regression:** announce the
  direction, and bring the cones in to 2 m.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus a 2017 article in the *Journal of Human
  Kinetics* comparing split-step movement speed in professional and junior players,
  read on 4 September 2026. The link works.
- **Worth a second look:** the words put the feeder at "the opposite service line"
  (across the net) but the drill is booked for **half a court** and the picture puts
  him on the player's own side, about 4.5 m in front. Those are two different drills
  to set up. Which did you mean?

## 2. Red ball: catch, then rally

- **Category:** games for children. **Level:** beginner only.
- **Ages:** under-10 and under-12.
- **Length:** 10 minutes by default; 6 to 15.
- **Volume:** 3 rounds, 30 seconds' rest, no fixed repetition count. Medium intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 8 children, half a court.
- **Equipment:** red balls, cones, hoops, a mini net.
- **What the player does:** a mini court is coned off inside one half — 11 m long,
  5 m wide, mini net across the middle, a hoop each side as the bounce target. Round
  one, the children throw and catch across the net five times in a row. Round two,
  one throws and the other lets it bounce in the hoop and hits it back. Round three,
  both hit and count the contacts out loud as a team. Then celebrate the best count
  and change partners.
- **What it develops:** tracking and catching, racket control, cooperative rallying,
  hand-eye work, awareness of space. Success is one pair getting a six-contact rally
  in at least one of three rounds.
- **Progression:** drop the catch, count to ten, then score a point for every rally
  past six. **Regression:** allow two bounces, or catch every ball before hitting.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus the LTA's "Coaching LTA Youth" programme
  page, read on 4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** two things. First, the only outside source is a British
  governing-body **programme page** — a public overview of the LTA's stages, with no
  publication year — used to justify the red ball and the cooperative scoring for a
  Spanish academy. It may be perfectly fine; it is the thinnest citation in the set.
  Second, the drill allows **up to 8 children on half a court with one mini net**. In
  practice that is four pairs sharing one net. Workable or chaos?

## 3. Pre-serve routine anchor

- **Category:** mental. **Level:** all four.
- **Ages:** under-14 up to adult.
- **Length:** 10 minutes by default; 6 to 15.
- **Volume:** 25 repetitions, 2 sets, 45 seconds' rest. Marked **low** intensity.
- **Players / court:** 1 to 4 players, half a court.
- **Equipment:** balls.
- **What the player does:** serves from one side with a basket. A fixed four-step
  routine is written on a card at the fence — set the feet the same way, bounce the
  ball the same number of times at the same tempo, picture the ball landing on the
  chosen target, say one short phrase, then serve without pausing. A partner counts
  how many serves were preceded by the complete routine, in order.
- **What it develops:** a repeatable pre-point routine, self-talk, holding attention.
  Success is 23 of 25 serves with the full routine, and a first-serve landing rate no
  worse than the player's own normal.
- **Progression:** run the routine inside a tie-break, and keep it identical after a
  double fault. **Regression:** practise the four steps without hitting, then add the
  serve.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus a 2022 *Frontiers in Psychology* study on
  imagery and self-talk before serving — which found gains in accuracy rather than
  speed, and that is why the drill scores where the serve lands, not how hard it is
  hit. Read 4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** three things.
  - **The serve count.** 25 serves × 2 sets is 50 serves; the app is allowed to run
    up to 40 × 3, which is **120 serves**. The drill is labelled *low* intensity —
    true of the mental effort, but 120 serves is a real shoulder load for a
    14-year-old, and nothing in the drill caps it.
  - **Equipment not listed.** The setup needs a **card at the fence** and the
    picture shows a **cone** and two **targets**; only "balls" is listed. Whoever
    sets the court up from the equipment list will be short.
  - **Where it can be placed.** The file allows this drill into a session's
    **cool-down** block. A serving-routine drill at the end of a session, as the
    cool-down — is that what you want?

## 4. Bodyweight court circuit (under-14)

- **Category:** physical. **Level:** beginner and intermediate.
- **Ages:** under-12 and under-14.
- **Length:** 12 minutes by default; 8 to 18.
- **Volume:** 8 repetitions, 3 sets (rounds), 60 seconds' rest. Medium intensity.
- **Players / court:** 1 to 8 players, half a court.
- **Equipment:** cones.
- **Supervision:** **requires a qualified strength-and-conditioning professional.**
- **What the player does:** four cones mark a 6 m square inside one half. Station 1,
  8 bodyweight squats, slow down, stand tall. Station 2, 8 split squats each leg,
  knee tracking over the toes. Station 3, 20 seconds' front plank, steady breath.
  Station 4, 8 low skater hops, landing quietly on one leg. Jog the square back to
  station 1; 60 seconds between rounds. The file is explicit: **no added load of any
  kind**, technique first, repetitions second.
- **What it develops:** lower-body strength, core stability, coordination, change of
  direction. Success is three rounds with knees tracking over the toes and no
  repetition finished out of balance.
- **Progression:** one more round, or slow the lowering phase to three seconds —
  **never** added load at this age. **Regression:** halve the repetitions, shorten
  the square to 4 m, hold a cone for balance on the single-leg work.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus a 2016 *Frontiers in Physiology* paper on
  resistance training in youth athletes (qualified supervision, technique before
  intensity), and a 2025 systematic review in *Sports* on injury risk in adolescent
  and adult players (workload spikes as a leading risk — which is why this circuit
  adds rounds rather than load). Both read 4 September 2026. This is the
  best-sourced drill in the set.
- **Worth a second look:** three things.
  - **Its title says under-14; its age list includes under-12.** In practice that
    means children as young as about eleven doing single-leg skater hops. Deliberate?
  - **The app may place it as a warm-up.** The file allows this circuit into either
    the *warm-up* or the *technical* block. A 12-minute strength circuit as the
    warm-up before a session is a choice worth making on purpose.
  - **The skater hop is a jump-and-land.** The file tags this drill as lower-body
    strength but not as jump-landing work. Because it is tagged as strength, the
    supervision requirement applies either way, so nothing is unprotected — but if
    you consider the skater hop plyometric, the file does not say so.

## 5. Return: read the toss, block deep middle

- **Category:** serve and return. **Level:** intermediate, advanced, high performance.
- **Ages:** under-14 up to adult.
- **Length:** 12 minutes by default; 8 to 20.
- **Volume:** 24 repetitions, 2 sets, 60 seconds' rest. Medium intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 4 players, a full court.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones, targets.
- **What the player does:** the server hits first serves only, alternating wide and
  T with no pattern. Cones mark a middle corridor two racket lengths either side of
  the centre mark, behind the service line. The returner stands one step behind the
  baseline and, as the toss reaches its peak, **calls "wide" or "T" out loud**. Then
  splits on the server's contact and blocks the ball deep through the middle. The
  coach records the call and the landing zone for ten serves; then they swap.
- **What it develops:** anticipation off the toss and shoulder turn, the blocked
  return, return position, the split step. Success is 7 of 10 calls correct and 6 of
  10 returns landing in the deep middle corridor.
- **Progression:** add a second server with a different toss and grip, then play the
  point out. **Regression:** the server announces wide or T, so only the block and
  the recovery are trained.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus a 2018 *Frontiers in Psychology* qualitative
  study of what professional returners read (the toss as a leading cue, and practise
  against varied servers — which is where the progression comes from). Read
  4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** **the server's workload is invisible here.** The drill
  scores the returner and counts nothing the server does, yet running it as written
  means one player hits 24 first serves per set, twice — and the app may run it at up
  to 40 × 4, which is **160 first serves** for whoever is serving. Neither the drill
  nor the app counts that against anything. For a pair of 14-year-olds swapping
  roles, that is the number to think about before you approve it.

## 6. Cross-court battle, first change wins

- **Category:** tactics. **Level:** intermediate, advanced, high performance.
- **Ages:** under-14 up to adult.
- **Length:** 14 minutes by default; 10 to 24.
- **Volume:** 12 rallies, 2 sets, 60 seconds' rest. **High** intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 4 players, a full court.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones.
- **What the player does:** both players rally cross-court from the baseline, counting
  the balls out loud. A cone each side marks the down-the-line corridor. From the
  fourth ball onwards either player may change direction — but the change only scores
  if it lands beyond the service line inside that corridor, and the player who changed
  must then win the point.
- **What it develops:** shot tolerance, changing direction at the right moment, depth
  control, reading the pattern. Success is 5 of 12 rallies won by whoever changed,
  with no change attempted before the fourth ball.
- **Progression:** raise the minimum to six cross-court balls, and let the opponent
  change first. **Regression:** drop the minimum to two, and let the change be a
  rally ball rather than an attacking one.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus the 2023 *PLOS ONE* elite men's singles match
  analysis — rally-length distributions and where finishing shots land, which is where
  the four-ball threshold comes from. Read 4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** the four-ball threshold is the whole drill, and it comes
  from **professional men's rally lengths**. Is four the right number for an under-14?
  Also: high intensity, up to 24 minutes, 12 live rallies played out — that is a
  substantial live-ball block for the youngest age band it is offered to.

## 7. Serve plus one into the open court

- **Category:** tactics. **Level:** intermediate, advanced, high performance.
- **Ages:** under-14 up to adult.
- **Length:** 15 minutes by default; 10 to 25.
- **Volume:** 24 repetitions, 2 sets, 60 seconds' rest. **High** intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 4 players, a full court.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones, targets.
- **What the player does:** the server plays from the deuce court at a cone marking
  the wide serve target inside the service box. The returner blocks the serve back
  cross-court — deliberately, not competitively. The server steps in and drives the
  second ball into a coned-off open-court zone in the opposite corner behind the
  service line. Ten sequences, count the good second balls, swap roles.
- **What it develops:** serve placement, two-shot point construction, court position,
  the forehand drive. Success is 6 of 10 second balls in the open-court zone, without
  the server crossing the baseline early.
- **Progression:** play the sequence out live, then repeat from the advantage court
  with a serve into the body. **Regression:** hand-feed a ball copying the wide
  return, so only the second ball is rehearsed.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus the same 2023 *PLOS ONE* match analysis —
  cited here for most points ending within four shots and first serves dominating
  them, which is why the drill rehearses two shots rather than a rally. Read
  4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** **serve volume again, and higher.** 24 serves × 2 sets is
  48 attacking first serves; the app may run 40 × 4, which is **160**. This is the
  highest-volume serving drill in the set, it is marked high intensity, and it starts
  at under-14. Nothing in the file limits the total serves a session may ask for.

## 8. Short ball: attack and close

- **Category:** tactics. **Level:** intermediate, advanced, high performance.
- **Ages:** under-14 up to adult.
- **Length:** 12 minutes by default; 8 to 20.
- **Volume:** 20 repetitions, 2 sets, 60 seconds' rest. **High** intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 4 players, half a court.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones, targets.
- **What the player does:** the feeder alternates one deep ball and one short ball. A
  cone on the service line is the recognition line — the player may only attack a ball
  that bounces in front of it. The player rallies the deep ball, attacks the short
  one, approaches, splits before the opponent's contact, and volleys into a marked
  target zone. Reset behind the baseline; ten attacks.
- **What it develops:** transition to the net, court position, forehand and backhand
  volley. Success is 6 of 10 attacks finishing with the first volley played from inside
  the service box, feet split before contact.
- **Progression:** make the feed random, then play the point out live after the first
  volley. **Regression:** announce the short ball, and let the approach land anywhere
  deep.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus the 2023 *PLOS ONE* match analysis (how many
  points finish in the service-box area, hence scoring the volley from inside the box)
  and the 2017 split-step article (split-step speeds including at the net, hence the
  split-before-contact requirement). Both read 4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** nothing beyond the two general points — it leans on the
  same men's-singles paper as the other tactics drills, and it is high intensity from
  under-14 up. Of the eight non-physical drills, this one is the most internally
  consistent: the words, the numbers and the picture agree.

## 9. Forehand depth ladder

- **Category:** technique. **Level:** intermediate and advanced.
- **Ages:** under-14 up to adult.
- **Length:** 12 minutes by default; 8 to 20.
- **Volume:** 30 repetitions, 3 sets, 45 seconds' rest. Medium intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 4 players, half a court to a full one.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones, targets.
- **What the player does:** the feeder stands at the centre of the far baseline and
  sends ten balls to the player's forehand corner at rally pace. Two cones mark a
  depth line 3 m inside the far baseline; the scoring zone is the cross-court corridor
  between that line and the baseline. The player hits every ball cross-court aiming
  past the depth line, and recovers behind the centre mark between each one.
- **What it develops:** the topspin forehand and the drive, depth control, a stable
  contact point. Success is 7 of 10 balls in the cross-court depth zone across two
  consecutive sets.
- **Progression:** feed wider and deeper, then swap to a live cross-court rally where
  only balls past the depth line count. **Regression:** feed from mid-court more
  slowly and move the depth line up to the service line until the contact point
  settles.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus the same 2023 *PLOS ONE* match analysis —
  cited here for where points finish and how often short rallies decide them, hence
  scoring depth rather than the line. Read 4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** the words say the depth line sits **3 m inside** the far
  baseline; the picture puts the cones about **3.9 m** inside it. A metre is a
  meaningful difference in a depth drill, and the two do not agree. Which is right?

## 10. Progressive rally warm-up

- **Category:** warm-up and cool-down. **Level:** all four.
- **Ages:** under-12 up to adult.
- **Length:** 12 minutes by default; the app may run it from 8 to 18.
- **Volume:** one set, no repetitions, no rest. Low intensity.
- **Players / court:** 2 to 8 players, half a court to a full one.
- **Equipment:** balls, cones.
- **What the player does:** four stages of three minutes. Three minutes jogging the
  tramlines, arm circles, hip openers, side shuffles. Three minutes mini-tennis inside
  the service boxes, soft hands, a split step on every ball. Three minutes at
  mid-court, medium pace, controlled rally. Three minutes at the baseline, building to
  rally tempo and finishing with four serves. Cones mark the three positions the pair
  retreats to.
- **What it develops:** mobility, glute and rotator-cuff activation, the split step,
  and a gradual raise. Success is all four stages completed in order, rallying at
  match tempo by the end, with no stage skipped. The stated objective is that no ball
  is hit at full intensity before the last stage.
- **Progression:** add a fifth stage of four serves at three-quarter pace before the
  first competitive point. **Regression:** keep both players at the service line and
  cut each stage to two minutes.
- **Sources:** the in-house entry, plus the 2025 systematic review in *Sports* on
  injury risk in adolescent and adult players — progressive load ramping, trunk and
  shoulder preparation. Read 4 September 2026.
- **Worth a second look:** **the script and the clock disagree.** The drill is four
  fixed stages of three minutes — twelve minutes — but the app is allowed to run it
  anywhere from 8 to 18 minutes. At 8 minutes the four stages do not fit, and the
  file does not say which stage to shorten or drop. The regression halves the stages
  to two minutes each, which gets you to 8; nothing says the app must use it. Worth
  deciding what a coach should cut when the court time is short, because the drill's
  own "common errors" list warns against exactly that.

## How to approve these

Aleksandr, you do not do any of this — you only say which ones. The manager does the
rest on your word. It is one small change and one pull request for all ten.

**Step 1 — the decision.** For each of the ten, say yes or no. A no needs no
explanation, though the reason helps whoever rewrites it. A "yes but change X" is
fine too; that is a rewrite, then a fresh look.

**Step 2 — the edit.** For each drill you approve, the manager changes **one word**
on **line 6** of its file: `status: reviewed` becomes `status: approved`. The ten
files, in the order above:

| # | Drill | File |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Split-step reaction gate | `content/drills/footwork/split-step-reaction-gate.yaml` |
| 2 | Red ball: catch, then rally | `content/drills/games_kids/red-ball-catch-and-rally.yaml` |
| 3 | Pre-serve routine anchor | `content/drills/mental/pre-serve-routine-anchor.yaml` |
| 4 | Bodyweight court circuit (under-14) | `content/drills/physical/bodyweight-court-circuit-u14.yaml` |
| 5 | Return: read the toss, block deep middle | `content/drills/serve_return/return-read-the-toss.yaml` |
| 6 | Cross-court battle, first change wins | `content/drills/tactics/cross-court-battle-first-change.yaml` |
| 7 | Serve plus one into the open court | `content/drills/tactics/serve-plus-one-open-court.yaml` |
| 8 | Short ball: attack and close | `content/drills/tactics/short-ball-attack-and-close.yaml` |
| 9 | Forehand depth ladder | `content/drills/technique/forehand-depth-ladder.yaml` |
| 10 | Progressive rally warm-up | `content/drills/warmup_cooldown/progressive-rally-warmup.yaml` |

Nothing else in any file changes. A drill you reject keeps `status: reviewed` and
stays out of the app.

**Step 3 — check the files are still valid.** From `server/`:

```bash
npm run content:validate
```

**Step 4 — the pull request.** One commit, one pull request, ten one-word changes.
Reviewed and merged the ordinary way.

**Step 5 — load them into the live app.** This is the step people forget, and it does
not happen by itself. Deploying the site runs the database migrations and starts the
API — it does **not** load drills. Someone has to run the import against the live
database once, from inside the running API container:

```bash
npm run content:import      # inside the api container, from server/
```

Only `approved` drills are loaded by that command, which is the whole point: until
step 2 happens, it loads nothing.

**Two footnotes, so nobody is surprised.**

- Another project document says approval is "a deliberate human decision inside the
  app". There is no such screen. Nothing in the app approves a drill, and the file
  edit above is the only way it happens today.
- Approving all ten does not by itself guarantee the session builder starts working.
  It refuses to build for more than one reason — an empty library is one, but so is
  "no drill in the library covers every age in this group" and "this group has fewer
  courts than the narrowest drill needs". It explains which reason applies each time.
  The first real session built after step 5 is the test of whether the ten are enough.
